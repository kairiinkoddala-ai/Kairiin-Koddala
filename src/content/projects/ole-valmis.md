## The Team

**Research Sprint:** Hanna Milk, Johannes Adrik, Gabriella Tarja, Kairiin Koddala

**Product Development & Systems Design:** Kairiin Koddala (Solo)

## The Problem

The Estonian Women's Voluntary Defence Organization (Naiskodukaitse) approached us with a clear problem: young people simply aren't downloading or using their Ole Valmis! crisis preparedness app. We framed our initial research sprint around a foundational question: *Why are young adults failing to prepare for crises before they happen?*

Our research revealed that young users face immense cognitive overload when looking at the app. The existing system felt like an intimidating, text-heavy manual rather than an actionable tool.

## Taking It Further: The Double Diamond Pivot

While the initial research was a team effort, I decided to take the project through the second half of the Double Diamond process entirely on my own. My goals were twofold: to solve the friction points uncovered in our research and to aggressively develop my own design system and interactive components inside Figma.

I ran an extensive round of usability testing on the live app and uncovered these core interface failures:

- **Overwhelming Scope** — Users were met with massive, endless laundry lists of survival gear right from the start, causing them to close the app out of anxiety.
- **Intimidating Visual Language** — The primary color choices (harsh police blues and blood reds) felt aggressive and alarming, inducing anxiety rather than a feeling of safe guidance.
- **Lack of Real-World Flexibility** — The app assumed every user had the budget and space to buy premium, specific crisis gear, offering zero alternatives.

## The Solution

I built a contextual, micro-interaction-driven prototype that heavily reduces initial choice paralysis.

- **Visual Refactoring** — I re-engineered their branding. I retained the core brand colors but introduced high-white tints to create soft, approachable, and calming pastel tones that don't trigger emergency panic.
- **Onboarding & Progression** — Instead of a massive list, the app now starts with a minimalist "Starter Kit" workflow. Once the basics are checked off, the user unlocks advanced levels.
- **Contextual & Inclusive Content** — I introduced alternative choices within the checklists, specifically adding budget-friendly and alternative options so the tool fits a student's real-life constraints.

## The Mistake I Made

I originally assumed users under stress wanted an un-opinionated, cold, minimalist checklist to complete as fast as possible. I stripped out descriptive context to save screen real estate, thinking it made the UI cleaner.

## The Pivot

During a live user test, a participant looked at a checklist item for a 'trash bag' and snapped: *"Why do I need a trash bag? I'm not sorting recycling if there's an active war."*

This completely flipped my perspective. I realized users needed micro-education in high-stress states. I redesigned the system component to clearly state why the item matters — a trash bag doubles as an immediate raincoat or ground insulation barrier. The pivot changed a simple list into a lifesaver, winning a direct production validation check from Naiskodukaitse's developers.
